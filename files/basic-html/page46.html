         I STOPPED USING streaming services a year and a half
         ago when I realized that not only did I not even enjoy the
         service I was paying for, but no one was making me stay.
         I remembered that I didn’t have to use any platforms to
         listen to music, I could get it straight from the source. I
         googled the newest ways to download music “legally”,
         found a method that didn’t seem too labor intensive, and
         cancelled my Spotify subscription. (I also bought an iPod
         on Ebay, but that’s besides the point.) I missed having to
         actually ﬁ nd music. I missed the effort it takes, having to
         download an album three different times because I got the
         wrong versions. I missed owning my music. I like down-
         loading an album I’ve never heard by an artist I don’t know,
         and falling in love with a world that I never knew existed.
         I like that I don’t need to have cell service to listen to it.
         Spotify, and all of its conveniences, has killed connection.
           Spotify’s worst crime is convincing us that we do indeed
         need it. Whenever I have to tell someone how I listen to
         music, I’m often met with confusion. “How do you listen,
         then,” they may say, as if we are forced to give a company
         money to even get access. Spotify, and the rest of the
         streaming world, have done such a good job at integrating
         themselves into people’s lives that they’ve erased the
         possibility of a world without them. The polished, stream-
         lined algorithms of Spotify make any other method seem
         tedious in comparison. The real issue here is that Ek saw
         piracy as a problem to solve, rather than a symptom of
         a bigger issue that he and his company are complicit in
         perpetuating.
           I think back to LimeWire, Napster, and even the newer
         tools that I use today, and I know that piracy has never
         been about theft, but autonomy and assuming control
         over the art that we consume. In a world where we have
         access to everything but own nothing, those small acts
         feel radical. Art isn’t something that we interact with any-
         more, but something to scroll past and forget about. Own-
         ership makes music deliberate. It makes it matter.
